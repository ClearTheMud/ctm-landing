Statement

It is an honor to serve as your Assessor. Throughout my time in office, I have worked to educate taxpayers about how the property tax system works and why taxes increase. Effective leadership requires an Assessor who understands the system, communicates openly, and works closely with taxpayers and elected representatives.

In Olympia, I successfully fought to expand the Senior Exemption Program, helping more low-income seniors receive the property tax relief they need to remain in their homes. Beginning this year, the income threshold will increase to $85,000, making many more seniors eligible. As participation grows, experienced leadership will be essential to ensure taxpayers receive relief quickly and efficiently.

I have also worked to advance a homestead exemption in Washington State and will continue working with fellow Assessors and legislators to provide meaningful property tax relief for hardworking homeowners.

Our office has led the way in using technology to ensure fair and equitable assessments while keeping costs low for taxpayers. Today, we operate with fewer full-time employees than when I was first elected while continuing to meet the growing needs of Clark County taxpayers. I respectfully ask for your vote.

Finding 6.1: State auditor found the assessor's office violated state law in a 2013 tax reduction that benefited the parents of an office employee MODERATE

What happened
A December 2015 Washington State Auditor's Office accountability audit found that the Clark County Assessor's Office, under Peter Van Nortwick, violated state law when it granted an $83,078 reduction in assessed value in 2013 to William and Genevieve Traub for land south of Ridgefield. The Traubs are the parents of Willy Traub, the levy manager in the assessor's office. Van Nortwick treated the change as a correction of a "manifest error," which state law limits to factual errors that do not require appraisal judgment. The auditor concluded the correction was instead made "using appraisal judgment" and therefore did not qualify, granting the Traubs tax relief they were not entitled to, with the shortfall shifted to other property owners the following year. The county faced no monetary penalty; the auditor directed the office to comply with the manifest-error statute going forward.

Defense
Van Nortwick maintained that the property was genuinely misassessed because of an error in a valuation table and that correcting it was proper. The dispute turned on a technical statutory line between a "manifest error" correction and a revaluation requiring appraisal judgment, not on a finding that Van Nortwick personally profited. The audit imposed no penalty and required only prospective compliance, and the employee relationship was familial to a staff member rather than a direct interest of the assessor.
