Methodology

What We Publish, What We Leave Out, and Why

TLP:GREEN Last updated: 2026-07-26
In short

We publish only what we can tie to a public record. If you have heard something about a candidate and you do not see it here, that usually means we could not document it well enough to print it, not that we decided it was untrue. An empty page is not a clean bill of health, and a long page is not proof of wrongdoing.

1 Why isn’t the thing I heard about on here?

This is the question we get most, so it goes first. You saw a post, or a neighbor told you something, and our page on that candidate says nothing about it. There are a handful of reasons, and none of them are that we are protecting anyone.

Before the list: if you know something we have missed, please send it to us. Several of the reasons below come down to us not having found the record yet, and readers are often the ones who know where it is.

We could not tie it to a record

We publish from documents: court filings, campaign finance reports, government records, and reporting by news organizations. If a claim exists only as a social media post, a forwarded screenshot, or something someone heard secondhand, we have nothing to check it against. We may well have looked into it and come up empty. We do not print things we cannot show you the source for.

Only one source has it

A single outlet, a single blog post, or a single person making an accusation is not enough on its own. For claims that come from news reporting rather than official records, we look for two or more independent outlets that reported it separately. One story that everyone else merely repeated is still one source.

It turned out to be resolved, or resolved in the candidate’s favor

Charges get dropped. Lawsuits get dismissed. Eligibility challenges get rejected by a judge. When a dispute ended in a candidate’s favor, repeating the original accusation on their page would leave you with the accusation and not the outcome. We do not do that. Where the dispute itself shaped the race, we describe it on the race overview page as background, with how it ended.

It is real, but it is not about the job

Plenty of true things about a person are nobody’s business at the ballot box. We ask whether a reasonable person deciding how to vote would find it materially relevant: legal trouble, a financial conflict with the office, a contradiction between what they promised and what they did, or a gap in the qualifications the job requires. A candidate’s family, health, faith, or private life is not on that list.

It is a feature of the race, not of the candidate

Some things that sound damaging are just how small-county politics works. Running against your own boss. Holding two local board seats at once. Having to resign one office if you win another. Running as the minority party in a county that leans the other way. These describe a situation, not a shortcoming, and we do not list them as concerns about a person.

We have not gotten to that race yet

We do not have equal depth everywhere. Some races have a full research write-up. Others have only the basic filing information from the state. If a page looks thin, check whether it is marked as a deep-dive or as filing data only. Thin often means unresearched, not investigated and cleared.

2 Where our information comes from

Every claim we publish is tagged with the kind of source it came from. We use five levels, and we only publish the top three.

Source levels and how we treat them
Level What it means Do we publish it?
T0, Primary Legal Court records, statutes, official legal filings Yes
T1, Official Record Government records: candidate filings, campaign finance reports, agency documents Yes
T2, Multi-Source Media Reported independently by two or more news organizations Yes
T3, Candidate Direct The candidate’s own website, campaign materials, or statements about themselves Only as “the candidate says,” never as established fact
T4, Single Source or Unverified One outlet, one person, or a claim we could not corroborate No

We do collect T4 material. We just do not print it. Something sitting at T4 in our files is not a secret we are keeping from you, it is a claim that did not survive checking, and publishing it would spread it without evidence.

3 What counts as a finding

A finding is a documented fact that a voter would reasonably weigh when deciding how to vote. To publish one we require an adequate source level and material relevance to the office being sought.

These are the categories we specifically do not publish as concerns about a candidate, even when the underlying fact is true:

  • Having no campaign website, no social media, or little online presence. This is ordinary, especially in small counties.
  • A party label, or running in a county that leans the other way.
  • Strengths. Being the leading fundraiser or a long-serving incumbent is not a concern, and we will not dress it up as one.
  • Raising less money than an opponent.
  • Having less experience than an opponent, on its own. Where a job carries a legal minimum qualification, meeting or missing it is a different matter and we do report that.
  • Routine dynamics of the race itself, including who a candidate works for.
  • Holding more than one local office, or needing to resign one to take another.
  • A single letter to the editor, which is one person’s opinion.
  • Anything that only works if we say it “may be” or “could be” a problem. If we cannot state it plainly, we do not publish it.

4 Why you cannot compare the counts

When a race page shows one candidate with several findings and another with none, that gap is not a scorecard. It is shaped heavily by how public each candidate’s job is.

Someone who currently holds office produces a public record constantly. Their decisions get covered by reporters. Incidents in their agency generate reports. Their employees file grievances. Members of the public file records requests about them. Someone who has never held office produces almost none of that, no matter what kind of person they are. Years in a public job create years of documents to find.

So a higher count can mean a candidate has been more exposed to public scrutiny rather than that they have done more wrong, and a lower count can mean there is simply less on the record to examine. We work to look in the places where a non-officeholder does leave a record, but we will not pad one candidate’s page to balance another’s. Read the findings themselves and judge their substance. Do not read the totals as a ranking.

5 What we do not do

  • We do not endorse candidates, and we do not accept payment from campaigns.
  • We do not decide which side a fact helps before deciding whether to publish it. The same standard is applied to every candidate in a race.
  • We do not remove a documented, adequately sourced finding because a campaign asks us to. We do correct anything that is wrong.
  • We do not publish private information about candidates or about private individuals who appear in records.

6 Tell us what we got wrong, and what we missed

Write to contact@clearthemud.org. We want both kinds of message, and the second kind is easy to talk yourself out of sending.

Errors

If something on this site is wrong, tell us. We make mistakes. When we do, we fix the page and say what changed, on the page itself, with the date. We do not quietly edit and move on. Candidates and their campaigns are welcome to write to us directly, and we read what they send.

Omissions

If you know of something about a candidate that we have not covered, send it. You do not need to be a reporter, and you do not need to have proof in hand. A great deal of what belongs in a public record is known first by the people who live in the county, work in the agency, or sat in the courtroom. If you can point us toward where a record lives, we can often go get it ourselves.

What helps most:

  • A link, a case number, a document, a date, or the name of the agency that would hold the record.
  • Which candidate and which race, so we can find the right page.
  • Roughly when it happened.

Two things to expect. First, we verify before we publish, so there will usually be a delay, and public records requests can take agencies weeks or months to answer. Second, we may look into something and still not publish it, because it did not meet the standards on this page. That is not a judgment about you or about whether it happened. We will not print something we cannot document.

Please do not send anything you obtained by breaking the law or a confidentiality obligation, and do not send private information about people who are not candidates.
